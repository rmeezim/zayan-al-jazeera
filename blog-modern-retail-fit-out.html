Fit-Out

How to plan a retail fit-out that opens on time.

By Zayan Al-Jazeera · 12 June 2026 · 7 min read

Every retail fit-out has one date that matters more than all the others: the day the doors open. Leases are signed against it, marketing is booked around it, staff are hired to meet it, and stock is ordered to fill the shelves on it. Miss that date and the costs are not just inconvenient — they are commercial, and they compound by the day. Yet most overruns are not caused by the build itself. They are caused by decisions made too late, surveys skipped, and long-lead items ordered weeks after they should have been. A fit-out that opens on time is planned to open on time, long before anyone picks up a tool.

Start with the date, then work backwards

The opening date is fixed. Treat it as the one immovable point in the whole programme and build everything else in reverse from it. Take the handover date you have promised the client, subtract the time you genuinely need for snagging and commissioning, and keep working backwards through finishes, services, first fix, demolition and protection until you arrive at the day you must start on site. If that start date is in the past, you have a problem to solve now — not in week six.

In a mall or a managed scheme, your real start is rarely the day you sign. Landlord access, the mall's handover condition, induction and permit approvals, and the centre's restricted delivery and working windows all eat into the front of the programme. Confirm those constraints in writing before you commit to a date, because they are the hardest to change once you are on site.

Survey before you specify

Drawings lie, kindly and often. The shell you were handed on a PDF is almost never the shell you find when you lift the ceiling tiles. Measure the real space — floor to soffit, column positions, the actual line of the demise — before a single material is specified or ordered. A bespoke shopfront cut to the drawing dimensions, not the as-built ones, is an expensive lesson learned at the worst possible moment.

A proper survey checks the things that quietly govern the build: the location and capacity of incoming services, the structure you can and cannot fix to, fire exits and the routes that must stay clear, slab levels and any falls, and the condition of what you are inheriting. Finding a redundant riser, an undersized supply or a structural beam where you wanted an opening is far cheaper to discover on day one than during second fix. Surveying early is not a cost; it is the cheapest insurance on the job.

Protect trading hours

If the unit next door is open — or if you are refitting around a store that must keep trading — the programme has to bend around the till, not the other way around. Phasing the works, segregating the live area and planning the noisy, dusty and disruptive tasks for the right time of day is what keeps the scheme's management onside and the neighbours from complaining you off site.

  • Programme noisy works — core drilling, demolition, heavy cutting — for overnight or out-of-hours slots agreed with centre management.
  • Erect proper hoarding with a clean, branded face so the works are screened and the public sees a coming-soon, not a building site.
  • Control dust at source with extraction and screens, and protect shared finishes, shopfronts and travelators every single shift.
  • Manage noise to the centre's decibel limits and keep a logged record of when restricted works were carried out.
  • Plan deliveries to the loading bay's booking system and keep escape routes and shared corridors clear at all times.
A deadline is a design constraint, not an afterthought. Plan as if the opening date is a load-bearing wall — because it is.

Order the long-lead items first

The build rarely runs late. The deliveries do. Bespoke joinery, structural and feature glazing, specialist and architectural lighting, and manufactured signage all carry lead times measured in weeks, sometimes months — and they almost always sit on the critical path to opening. A flawless shell with no shopfront in it does not open a store.

So order the long-lead items the moment the design is frozen, not when you reach that point in the programme. Confirm lead times in writing, build in contingency for the inevitable revision, and track procurement as rigorously as you track the labour. The trades on site can usually absorb a lost week. A reordered shopfront cannot.

Coordinate the trades, not just the trades' work

A fit-out is not a relay of separate trades each doing their bit in turn. It is a single piece of choreography where the electrician, the ductwork installer, the sprinkler fitter and the ceiling team are all competing for the same few hundred millimetres above the grid. Mechanical, electrical and plumbing coordination — resolved on a drawing before anyone is in the ceiling — is what separates a clean second fix from a fortnight of clashes, rework and finger-pointing.

Sequencing matters as much as the work itself: the right trade, in the right order, with the space prepared for the one that follows. And every fit-out needs a single point of accountability — one project manager who owns the programme, holds the trades to it and answers to the client. Gaps between trades are where time and quality fall through. Closing them is the job.

Leave real time for snagging and handover

When a programme slips, the end is always where teams try to claw it back — and it is exactly the wrong place to compress. Snagging, commissioning and the final clean are what turn a space that is practically complete into one that is genuinely trading-ready. Lights that flicker, a chip in the laminate, a door that drags — none of it is structural, and all of it is what the brand manager and the first customers will notice.

Protect that window. There is a real difference between practically complete and trading-ready, and the gap between them is filled with snagging, demonstrations, training and the paperwork that makes the handover stick: operation and maintenance manuals, test certificates, warranties and a clear aftercare commitment. Hand over a store that works on day one — and keep working long after the dust has settled.
